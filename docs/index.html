Can a computer tell a malignant tumour from a benign one?

A classifier trained on 569 microscope samples of breast lumps. This site walks through the data, the models, and every graph, with a plain-English note beside each one.

How to read this site. Use the menu on the left to jump around. Each graph in Graphs explained has three notes: What you're seeing, How to read it and What it tells us. In Try it yourself you can move the decision threshold and watch the results change.

The problem in one paragraph

A doctor takes a tiny needle sample from a breast lump. Software measures the cell nuclei in the microscope image, and the model predicts malignant (cancer) or benign (not cancer). The two mistakes are not equal: missing a cancer is far worse than an unnecessary follow-up test. That is why this project goes beyond accuracy and tunes the model to catch as many cancers as possible.

Step 1

The data

Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer dataset: 569 patients, 30 measurements each, no missing values. 212 are malignant (37.3%) and 357 benign (62.7%).

Needle sample

A fine needle takes cells from the breast lump (a fine-needle aspirate).

Microscope image

The cells are stained and photographed under a microscope.

Software measures

Every cell nucleus is outlined and 10 properties are measured for each one.

30 features

Each property is summarised 3 ways: mean, standard error, and "worst" (most extreme nuclei). 10 × 3 = 30.

The 10 measurements, in plain words

MeasurementWhat it means
RadiusTypical distance from the centre of the nucleus to its edge (size).
TextureHow much the brightness varies inside the nucleus. Even staining is low; a blotchy, clumpy interior is high.
Perimeter, AreaLength of the outline and the space inside it (more size measures).
SmoothnessHow much the radius varies locally; a rough outline scores higher.
CompactnessHow tightly packed the shape is (perimeter² ÷ area − 1).
ConcavityHow deep the inward dents in the outline are.
Concave pointsHow many separate inward dents there are.
SymmetryHow evenly the two halves of the nucleus match.
Fractal dimensionHow rough and complex the boundary is, like measuring a coastline.

Why does texture relate to cancer? Cancer-cell nuclei tend to be larger, irregular in shape, and have unevenly clumped chromatin (the DNA material), which appears as higher brightness variation. The model picked up a pattern that matches what pathologists look for. It is a statistical pattern in 569 patients, not proof that texture causes cancer.

Step 2

How it works

The workflow is built so the test set is used exactly once, at the very end. That keeps the final numbers honest.

Split

80% of patients to train (455), 20% held back for the final exam (114). Both keep the same cancer ratio.

Scale

Put all 30 features on a comparable scale, inside a pipeline so nothing leaks from validation data.

Compare

Six models, each scored with 5-fold cross-validation on the training set only.

Tune

Grid search tries different settings for the best candidates and keeps the best by F1.

Pick a threshold

Choose the cut-off that still catches at least 98% of cancers, using training-set predictions only.

Final exam

Score the chosen model once on the 114 unseen patients.

Explain

Permutation importance shows which measurements the model relies on.

Step 4

Graphs explained

Every figure from the project, with a plain-English reading guide. Click any graph to enlarge it.

Graph 1 · Exploring the data

Class balance and the strongest features

What you're seeing

Left: how many patients are benign (357) and malignant (212). Right: the 8 measurements most strongly linked to a malignant result.

How to read it

Longer bars on the right mean a stronger link (a correlation of 0 is no link, 1 is perfect). The classes are imbalanced, so accuracy alone can mislead.

What it tells us

Size and shape irregularity (concave points, perimeter, radius) are the clearest signals. But many features say the same thing: 21 pairs are correlated above 0.9.

Graph 2 · Comparing models

Which algorithm sorts patients best?

What you're seeing

The average F1 score of six models across 5 cross-validation folds. The grey lines are one standard deviation.

How to read it

F1 combines precision and recall into one number (1.0 is perfect). If the grey lines overlap, the difference between models may be luck.

What it tells us

All six land between 0.946 and 0.964 and their error bars overlap. Even simple logistic regression matches the neural network, so the classes are close to linearly separable.

Graph 3 · Choosing the cut-off

The precision–recall trade-off

What you're seeing

The model outputs a probability of malignancy. This graph shows precision (blue) and recall (orange) for every possible cut-off on that probability.

How to read it

Moving left means flagging more patients as "malignant": recall rises, precision falls. The dotted line is the default 0.5; the green dashed line is the chosen 0.14.

What it tells us

Because missing a cancer is the worse error, the threshold is set to keep recall at 98% or more, accepting more false alarms. Try it live in Try it yourself.

Graph 4 · The final exam

Confusion matrices and ROC curve on unseen patients

What you're seeing

Left and middle: counts of right and wrong predictions on 114 patients the model never saw, at two thresholds. Right: the ROC curve.

How to read it

The diagonal cells (top-left, bottom-right) are correct. The bottom-left cell is the dangerous one: a cancer predicted as benign. On the ROC curve, hugging the top-left corner is good.

What it tells us

At 0.5: 3 cancers missed, 0 false alarms. At 0.14: 1 missed, 6 false alarms. ROC-AUC is 0.993, so the model separates the two groups very well.

Graph 5 · Opening the black box

Which measurements matter most?

What you're seeing

For each feature, how much the model's test F1 drops when that feature's values are randomly shuffled (repeated 30 times).

How to read it

A longer bar means the model leaned on that feature more. A bar near zero means the model could get by without it.

What it tells us

Worst texture (0.051), worst concavity and mean concavity lead. Values are small because features are redundant: shuffle one and the model recovers the signal from its siblings.

Reference

Glossary

The terms used above, in plain English.

Precision

Of all the patients the model flagged as malignant, the share that really were. High precision means few false alarms.

Recall (sensitivity)

Of all the patients who really had cancer, the share the model caught. High recall means few missed cancers. It is the most important metric here.

F1 score

A single number that balances precision and recall. It is high only when both are high.

Accuracy, and why it can mislead

The share of all predictions that were right. With 63% benign cases, a model that always says "benign" would already score 63%, so accuracy alone hides the cost of missed cancers.

Confusion matrix

A 2×2 table of correct and wrong predictions: true negatives, false positives, false negatives and true positives.

ROC curve and AUC

The ROC curve shows how well the model ranks cancers above non-cancers across all thresholds. AUC is the area under it: 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.

Decision threshold

The model outputs a probability. The threshold is the cut-off above which a patient is labelled malignant. Lower it to catch more cancers at the cost of more false alarms.

Cross-validation

Split the training data into 5 parts, train on 4 and test on the 5th, rotate five times, and average. It gives a steadier estimate than one split.

Permutation importance

Shuffle one feature's values and measure how much performance drops. A big drop means the model depends on that feature.

Data leakage

When information from the test data sneaks into training, making results look better than they really are. This project avoids it by scaling inside the pipeline and choosing the threshold without the test set.

Be honest about it

Limitations, and how to run it

Not a medical device. This is a teaching project and must not be used for real diagnosis.

  • Small test set. With 114 patients, one patient moves recall by about 2.4 points, so "3 vs 1 missed cancers" is suggestive, not conclusive.
  • One dataset, one institution. Performance on other labs or scanners is untested.
  • Pre-computed features. The model sees measurements, not raw images; a CNN on the images would be a different, harder problem.
  • Untuned neural network. It could not be fairly ruled out against the SVM.

Reproduce the results

pip install -r requirements.txt
python run_project.py

The random seed is fixed at 42, so you get identical numbers every time. Running the script also regenerates this website's data.
